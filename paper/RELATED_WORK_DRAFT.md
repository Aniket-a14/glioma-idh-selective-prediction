Selective prediction and conformal prediction offer complementary ways to make medical-imaging models more useful when an automatic answer is not equally reliable for every case. Borges, Pacheco, and Silva (2024) study image-level selective prediction for semantic segmentation under distribution shift, introducing a confidence measure tailored to the Dice objective and showing, across three medical-imaging tasks, that post-hoc confidence estimation can reduce reliance on expert review. For classification, Hirsch and Goldberger (2024) propose learning an instance-dependent conformal threshold and then conformalizing it, producing prediction sets with the requested coverage while reducing average set size on standard medical-imaging datasets. Lu et al. (2023) extend conformal prediction to federated settings, replacing the usual exchangeability assumption with partial exchangeability to address heterogeneous clients and demonstrating the approach on medical-imaging data. Cheung et al. (2024) move conformal guarantees beyond class labels and pixels by constructing metric-guided bounds for reconstructed 3-D breast CT images used in radiotherapy planning; their experiments show that bounds aligned with downstream clinical metrics can achieve the target coverage where conventional pixel-wise bounds do not. Together, these studies motivate a post-hoc trustworthiness layer that can either abstain, return a set of plausible labels, or quantify uncertainty in a clinically meaningful output, while also highlighting the assumptions that must be checked: calibration coverage, distribution shift, heterogeneity across sites, and alignment between the uncertainty target and the downstream decision. The present study applies that perspective to MRI-based glioma IDH classification by evaluating selective risk and coverage alongside calibration rather than treating accuracy alone as sufficient evidence of reliability.

## Cross-dataset overlap and leakage in public brain-tumor MRI benchmarks

Public brain-tumor benchmarks are not automatically independent cohorts. Dorfner et al. (2025) identify BraTS 2021 as containing a subset of studies from TCGA-GBM, TCGA-LGG, IvyGAP, and CPTAC-GBM, while Suter et al. (2022) describe the same source collections when documenting the 1,251-patient BraTS 2021 training set. Dataset names therefore do not establish external independence: combining a TCGA-derived collection with BraTS can place the same patient in both sides of an apparent cross-dataset evaluation unless source provenance and subject identifiers are reconciled.

Recent studies show practical controls for this problem. Ren et al. (2025) combined five public glioma datasets but excluded overlapping patients between BraTS 2021 and the TCGA-LGG, TCGA-GBM, and UPENN-GBM subsets before analysis. Liu et al. (2026) used patient identifiers and metadata to deduplicate four public brain-tumor MRI collections, added perceptual hashing to flag near-identical images, and then assigned each subject to only one split. These workflows separate two related checks: provenance- and identifier-based patient matching across collections, followed by image-level redundancy screening when metadata are incomplete.

Leakage can also occur within one collection when correlated slices or repeated scans are split independently. Yağış et al. (2021) measured this effect in brain MRI classification and found that slice-level cross-validation inflated accuracy by 29–55% across several cohorts relative to subject-level evaluation. Although those cohorts were neurological rather than glioma datasets, the failure mode applies directly to slice-based tumor benchmarks. Credible glioma evaluation therefore requires patient-level splits, an explicit cross-collection overlap audit, and a manifest of excluded subjects before model fitting or external testing. The present study follows that approach by treating cohort provenance and patient identity as part of the evaluation design rather than as post-hoc metadata.
